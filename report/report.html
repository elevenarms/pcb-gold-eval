At a glance · The answer, in one line

Machines can connect a board. Nothing open yet routes it the way its engineers did, and the eval to prove it did not exist.

The literature has no learned PCB method that optimises impedance, skew or length matching; only vendors claim it. So the work started with the measuring stick: a Harbor eval that strips the copper from eight fabricated, Apache-licensed Antmicro boards and scores machine routing against the human original.

Five findings

  1. Search beats reinforcement learning per dollar, so far.Evolutionary and tree search won at hour scale in chip placement; RL’s headline result has no independent reproduction.
  2. Completion hides the high-frequency failure.The open router finishes 72–96% of simpler boards, stalls at 18% on a BGA board, and misses the skew, length and width targets everywhere.
  3. The evaluator is the bottleneck, not the optimiser.Proxy metrics barely predict final quality (Kendall τ 0.05–0.40); full-wave checks cost minutes per structure.
  4. Compute is cheap; wall clock and fidelity are not.A medium board’s routing candidate costs about one cent on CPU. Budgets matter only for RL, LLM evolution and full-wave EM on DDR-class boards.
  5. Gold masters turn opinion into a number.Antmicro’s length and skew targets exist only in their copper; the eval extracts them and makes every claim testable.

The question

Can self-improving machine learning route a high-frequency board as well as the engineers who shipped it?

The claim under review: reinforcement learning, evolutionary algorithms and deep networks that learn to place and route boards for high-frequency constraints, without hand-coded heuristics. Testing it needs three things the field lacks: graded evidence, an open tool stack, and a benchmark with physics in the score.

Exhibit 1The starting point: Zine Machine v3, routed by an agent with open tools

Best-so-far unconnected items on the v3 main board, 6 Nov 2026, from the Backplane transcripts. Ninety-two per cent of connections were made in 22 minutes; the last one took 2.5 hours. The 499 ceiling is an artefact: kicad-cli’s DRC report stops listing unconnected items at 499, a cap found while building this eval.

Why a new base case

The project board is a weak test

v3’s fastest interface is USB 2.0 and its parallel display bus runs near 30 MHz. It has no DDR, no PCIe and one routed history. A credible test needs boards with real high-speed interfaces, routed by professionals, built and used.

Scope

Open source only

Every tool, solver and dataset here is open source and runs headless in a pinned container. Commercial routers (Quilter, DeepPCB, Allegro X AI) appear only as context, graded as vendor claims.

Method

Four parallel workstreams

A graded literature review (30 sources), an inventory of 40 open tools with live repo data, an eval built and validated in Harbor, and a compute forecast with every parameter tagged by source.

Proven

GPU analytical placement is fast and good. Evolutionary and tree search beat pure RL at hour scale. LLM-evolved heuristics give single-digit gains at a known price. Surrogates reproduce SI metrics in narrow parameter spaces.

Not proven, and the gap

“Superhuman” RL placement; any learned PCB router that beats rule-based routers on real boards; any method that optimises high-frequency SI end to end. No PCB benchmark scores signal integrity, so none of it can yet be shown.

Scores from primary sources (READMEs, papers, release notes). GPL tools are called as separate processes so the rest of the stack stays permissive. 28 further tools were reviewed and left out: inactive, chip-only or no PCB path.

Fast loop

KiCadRoutingTools as the inner router, Freerouting as the reference, analytic impedance tables and skew/length checks as the cheap score. Many single-threaded processes, not one big machine.

Physics

openEMS through gerber2ems is the only open path from a PCB to S-parameters. Its GPU engine landed on master in October 2026 with no published speedups: benchmark it before budgeting GPUs.

Self-improvement

evosax or pymoo for routing recipes; OpenEvolve for router code; BoTorch to decide when a candidate earns a full-wave check. RL last, if at all.

First results

The open router connects the board and misses the physics.

Freerouting 2.5, run with no agent on the stripped boards, is the floor every method must beat. On boards without dense BGAs it completes 72–96% of connections, then fails exactly where high-frequency design lives: pair skew, length groups and impedance-correct widths. On the BGA-heavy HDMI bridge it stalls at 18%.

Independent runs overlap the gold copper by about 1% (gold_copy_fraction 0.008–0.012). Oracle: 8 of 8 tasks score 1.00 through Harbor.

What works Works well

The pipeline end to end: pinned inputs, strip, separate verifier, oracle at 1.00, and a gold-copy check that flags any run reproducing the human copper.

What the floor shows Refine

On the PCIe adapter the router made 96% of connections but only 1 of 9 pairs met the skew tolerance and 43% of high-speed copper used an impedance-correct width.

Next runs Coming soon

Agent runs through Harbor (Claude Code, Codex, OpenHands), KiCadRoutingTools with length matching, and the hard DDR tier with a full-wave check on sampled nets.
Integrity. Agents have network access, so a run could download the gold board. The verifier reports gold_copy_fraction, the share of segments that coincide with the human copper on a 10 µm grid; leaderboards disqualify runs above 0.5. Public boards may also sit in model training data, a contamination risk shared by every open benchmark.

Chapter 4 of 4 · Outcome against dollars and hours

Below a few hundred dollars per board, time and the evaluator decide the outcome

Exhibit 7Best achievable reward, hard tier (DDR5-class board)

Envelope over seven strategies; floor contours at 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 0.8. The step near $300 is the surrogate’s data bill; the ridge near one day is its training time.

How the forecast works

Each strategy has a fixed cost (training data, GPU fit), a cost and duration per candidate, a parallelism limit and a saturating return, G = g₀ + (g_max − g₀)(1 − (1 + N/n₀)−α), where N is the number of candidates the budget and the clock both allow. α = 0.6 and n₀ = 60 reproduce WireMask-EA’s diminishing returns. Starting points come from the measured baselines; ceilings for untested strategies are labelled assumptions. Prices: Modal CPU $0.047 per core-hour, A100 $2.50 per hour (7 Nov 2026). All parameters live in forecast/forecast.toml; make forecast redraws every chart.

Budgeting recommendations

Spend on parallel CPU and on the evaluator. Buy GPUs only after the solver earns them.

  1. Cap routing compute at about $100 per board.A medium-board candidate costs about one cent of CPU; $100 buys some 9,000 candidates, far past the point where search flattens.
  2. Put the next $1,000 into the evaluator.Build the SI tooling and an openEMS-trained surrogate for the hard tier; it is the only strategy whose ceiling rises with spend.
  3. Buy wall clock with cores, not GPUs.256 spot cores at about $4 an hour turn a one-week search into an overnight one. Benchmark openEMS’s new GPU engine before any GPU spend.
  4. Fund RL only as a research line.At about $3,000 and three days of fixed cost, it reaches less than search does today.
